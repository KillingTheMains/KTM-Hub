Read this before you fly anything. Truss Plot is a planning aid for the pre-production conversation with the venue and the rigger. It is not an engineering review, and every manufacturer table it uses says the same thing: point loads at panel points, no bridling from the truss, and cantilevers or anything past the table need the manufacturer or a licensed engineer. Fixture weights are published figures with omega brackets where the maker includes them; check the real thing on a scale.
What the numbers assume. Two hoists: exact statics, so an overhung end shows uplift on the far hoist when it happens. Three or more: the tributary split riggers use in practice — each hoist takes the load from the midpoint to its neighbours — and each span is checked as a simple beam between its two hoists, which is conservative for midspan and blind to hogging over the middle hoists. Truss capacity is compared by moment: the lower of the table's UDL and CPL rows at that span, interpolated between rows; a cantilever of length a is checked against the table at a span of 2a. Tomcat tables are Aluminum Design Manual figures not yet reduced for repetitive use — the ×0.85 is ANSI E1.2's touring factor and is applied to every table when selected, which is doubly conservative on Thomas tables that already carry 20 % for dynamics and on Prolyte's Eurocode tables. Hoist WLL is checked against the hook load times the dynamic factor; the load to the grid adds the hoist body, chain at the chain-lift length, and a bag.
Bridles aren't planned here. If a point lands where there's no steel, a two-leg bridle at 90° included puts 0.71× the point load in each leg; ASME B30.9 stops you at 120°. That's the venue rigger's call, not this page's.
